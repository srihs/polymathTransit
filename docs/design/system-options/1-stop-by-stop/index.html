Stop by Stop

Every journey is drawn as a line with stops. The same grammar runs through the whole system: on the form your trip is a line you build stop by stop, on the dashboard each van's day is a line along the clock, and on the driver's phone the route is a vertical strip of stops.

It is signage discipline applied to software: one typeface with a road-sign lineage, five sizes, two weights, black bands that carry structure, and shapes that always mean the same thing. Colour helps, but a shape and a word always carry the meaning.

Principles

  1. Draw the route, then label it. Order and connection come first.
  2. A shape means one thing everywhere. A crossbar is a stop; a ring is "proposed"; a diamond is "needs attention".
  3. One typeface, five sizes, two weights. Overpass, with Noto Sans Sinhala for Sinhala.
  4. Read it at walking pace: the next thing is always the biggest thing.

Two things never to do

  • Do not put crest.png on a ground darker than purple 200, and do not rebuild "Polymath College" in a font beside the crest.

3Colour

Purple is the route: the line, the primary action, the chosen option, links. Black carries structure. Every other colour has a job and never works alone: each status also has a shape and a word. Contrast is WCAG 2.2: 4.5:1 for text (AA), 7:1 (AAA), 3:1 for parts of the interface and large text.

4Type

One typeface: Overpass, an open-source interpretation of the Highway Gothic road-sign letters, in two weights (400 and 700). Sinhala is set in Noto Sans Sinhala, unmodulated and open like Overpass. All times use tabular figures, so they line up in columns. Letter-spacing is 0; nothing is set in capitals except reference codes.

The Sinhala size factor at work

Noto Sans Sinhala draws its letters larger than Overpass draws its lower case, so Sinhala is set at 0.95 of each size (--lf under :lang(si)) and given more line height (1.65 for body) for its tall vowel signs. Compare the label at both factors beside the English.

5Spacing and grid

An 8 px base unit with one half step. Corners are square on plates and panels and 4 px on inputs and buttons. There are no shadows: bands, rules and lines do the separating.

  • --s-2 8 px
  • --s-3 16 px
  • --s-4 24 px
  • --s-5 32 px
  • --s-6 48 px
  • --s-7 64 px

Phone grid: 4 columns, 16 px margins and gutters (360 px)

Phone pages sit in a centred column of at most 480 px on wider screens. Plates and the reference plate run to the column's edges.

Desktop grid: 12 columns, 24 px gutters, 1280 px maximum

Dashboard: the plan tile takes 4 columns and the four count tiles 2 each; the day timeline takes 8 columns and the detail panel 4. Below 1100 px they stack.

8Signature element: the route spine

A 6 px purple line with a crossbar at each stop and a wide terminal bar at the end. On the form it runs down "Your trip" from "Pick up from" to "Going to", and "+ Add a stop" inserts a new crossbar on the line, so the order of stops is visible before anything is sent (FR-54, BRL-18). On the dashboard it reads a proposal back as a run, with seats on board beside each stop. On the driver's phone it is the backbone of the route: travelled sections turn solid black and the next stop's marker is the largest mark on the screen. Its job is to show order, the one thing a multi-stop request can get wrong.

9Motion

Motion only explains a change in the route. Nothing loops, nothing moves on scroll, nothing spins. With "reduce motion" set on the device, every change is instant.
